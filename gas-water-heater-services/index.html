Most gas water heaters in Beaverton, OR work the same basic way. Gas enters through a control valve that doubles as the thermostat and feeds a burner beneath the tank, and the hot exhaust travels up a flue through the middle of the tank and out through the vent. A thermocouple, or a flame sensor on newer electronic models, tells the valve a flame is present; without that signal, the valve closes for safety. The design is dependable, but it has a few predictable weak spots. Thermocouples wear out, pilot tubes and burner ports clog, the flame arrestor screen at the base packs up with lint and dust, control valves fail, and vents lose their draft.

Beaverton Water Heater does not guess with gas appliances. On every Beaverton call, our technicians test the thermocouple's millivolt output, inspect the pilot and burner flame, check gas pressure at the valve, and run a draft test at the draft hood to confirm exhaust is actually leaving the house. We look inside the combustion chamber for scorching, rust, and signs of flame rollout, which tell us whether the flue is blocked or the burner is misbehaving, and we check for carbon monoxide around the heater. One rule comes before all of it: if you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the house, call your gas utility from outside, and then call us once it is safe.

The fix is usually straightforward once the cause is known. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • Popping, rumbling, or crackling sounds as the burner heats
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • A cold tank and a burner that will not come on
  • Hot water that runs out faster than it used to
  • Rust-colored hot water or rust streaks on the tank
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first

Why Professional Gas Water Heater Services Matters

Gas water heaters are among the riskiest appliances to work on without training, because the biggest hazards are invisible. A fitting that is almost tight can seep gas for days before anyone notices. A vent connector that has lost its slope, or a flue partly blocked by debris, can let carbon monoxide spill into the house every time the burner fires, with no smell to warn you. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically require a permit and an inspection. Relighting a pilot by following the label is something most homeowners can do; almost everything past that deserves a technician.

Our technicians approach gas heaters with instruments rather than assumptions. They measure thermocouple millivolts, read gas pressure at the valve, test draft at the hood, check for CO around the appliance, and leak-test every joint they touch. The result is a repair that targets the real fault and a home that is safer for it. We also give you an honest assessment of the heater itself. A tank past about ten or twelve years with a failing valve or a leak is usually a replacement, and we will say so before you spend money on a repair.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Here's where to begin:

  • Make sure your home has CO alarms that work, especially with gas appliances
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Check that the vent connector is secure and rising toward the chimney or flue
  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
