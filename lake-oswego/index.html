Keeping Your Lake Oswego Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling once a year in hard-water areas.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the month and year it was built, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units around 20. If your heater is beyond that range and is also showing discolored water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or a missing expansion tank, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can wear out the valve and stress the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Common Water Heater Problems in Lake Oswego

The majority of water heater failures we see in Lake Oswego start with what's in the water. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Age is the other big factor. Many homes around here still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and beyond about ten years, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust begins attacking the steel from the inside. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater frequently means updating the installation along the way: an expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, venting that drafts properly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, including extra sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that sometimes develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most cost far less to address early.
